**Deep Learning in Econometrics for Causal Inference and Structural Modeling (2020–2025)**

**Introduction**

In recent years, deep learning (DL) techniques – particularly deep neural networks – have begun to permeate econometric analysis, offering flexible nonlinear modeling capabilities that can complement traditional econometric methods. _Causal inference_ and _structural modeling_ are two areas of econometrics that have seen growing applications of deep learning since 2020. Causal inference traditionally relies on frameworks like potential outcomes and structural equations, often using parametric or tree-based models to estimate treatment effects. Structural econometric modeling involves specifying economic models (e.g. dynamic optimization or equilibrium systems) and estimating their parameters or solving for policy functions, which is often computationally intensive in high dimensions. Deep learning’s ability to approximate complex functions and handle high-dimensional data presents an opportunity to improve these tasks. However, until recently, deep learning has been **“conspicuously underutilized”** by social scientists for causal analysis ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20deep%20learning%20remains%20conspicuously,be%20configured%20with%20different%20architectures)), and only in the last few years have researchers systematically explored its use in econometric contexts. This systematic literature review (SLR) synthesizes the emerging body of work (2020–2025) at the intersection of deep learning, causal inference, and structural econometric modeling.

We organize the review as follows. First, we describe the SLR methodology, including search strategy, inclusion criteria, and the screening process (documented with a PRISMA flow diagram). Next, in the Results, we critically appraise the included studies, dividing them into two main categories: (1) applications of deep learning for causal inference, and (2) applications of deep learning for structural econometric modeling. For each study, we summarize the methodology, the type of deep learning technique used, the econometric context, datasets, key findings, and any noted limitations or potential sources of bias. A summary table is provided to allow quick reference to these details. Finally, we discuss gaps in the current literature and suggest areas for future research. All citations are given in APA style and key sources are referenced to maintain attribution.

**Methodology of the Systematic Review**

We followed established systematic review guidelines (PRISMA 2020) for identifying, screening, and selecting relevant literature ([The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews | Systematic Reviews | Full Text](https://systematicreviewsjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13643-021-01626-4#:~:text=the%20PRISMA%202020%20statement)). The review scope was defined to include publications from 2020 up to early 2025 that apply deep learning techniques in econometric analyses **with a focus on causal inference or structural modeling**. We included peer-reviewed journal articles, conference papers, working papers (e.g. NBER, arXiv preprints), and selected grey literature (such as institutional reports) to ensure comprehensive coverage. Both methodological papers (proposing new algorithms or theoretical results) and applied studies (using deep learning on econometric problems) were eligible, provided they addressed causal inference (e.g. treatment effect estimation, policy evaluation, causal discovery) or structural modeling (e.g. estimation of structural parameters, solving economic models).

**Data Sources and Search Strategy:** We searched scholarly databases and repositories including Google Scholar, EconLit, arXiv, SSRN, and NBER working papers. Key search terms combined concepts of deep learning (e.g. “deep neural network”, “deep learning”, “representation learning”) with econometrics and our focal domains (e.g. “causal inference”, “treatment effect”, “structural model”, “dynamic programming”, “instrumental variables”). Example queries were _“deep learning causal inference econometrics 2020”_ and _“neural network structural model estimation”_. We also examined bibliographies of relevant survey articles (if any) to identify additional sources (snowballing). The initial search (performed across multiple databases in March 2025) yielded over 500 candidate records after removing very old or clearly irrelevant items.

**Screening and Selection:** Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram of the screening process. We first removed duplicates across databases, yielding approximately 480 unique records. We then screened titles and abstracts for relevance. At this stage, we excluded works that did not explicitly use deep **neural** learning methods (for example, papers on “machine learning” in general that only used tree ensembles or other non-DL methods), as well as those not dealing with either causal inference or structural economic models. About 430 records were excluded in this step due to irrelevance or out-of-scope content (e.g. pure forecasting applications without causal/structural focus). We retrieved 50 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Each of these was read to ensure it indeed applied deep learning in a causal or structural econometric context and was published (or disseminated as a preprint) during 2020–2025. Some papers were excluded at this stage for reasons such as: lacking substantive econometric context (e.g. purely algorithmic papers with no linkage to causal or structural questions), being outside the timeframe, or not providing sufficient detail for appraisal. In total, **20 studies** met all criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis (review). Because the included studies were heterogeneous and many were not amenable to meta-analysis (owing to different goals and metrics), we focus on a narrative synthesis; no quantitative meta-analysis was performed (hence 0 studies in quantitative synthesis in Figure 1).

**Data Extraction and Quality Appraisal:** For each included study, we extracted details on: authors, year, title, the main methodology or model, the deep learning techniques utilized, the econometric context (causal inference or structural modeling application), data or datasets used (simulations or empirical), key results or findings, and noted limitations. To critically appraise quality and bias, we considered several dimensions: (a) methodological rigor (e.g. soundness of the study design or theoretical analysis), (b) validity of results (e.g. performance on benchmarks, use of appropriate evaluation metrics), (c) generalizability (whether results likely hold beyond the specific data or setting), and (d) potential conflicts of interest or other biases (noting if authors were evaluating their own methods without external comparison, etc.). We adapted risk-of-bias assessment criteria from common frameworks (e.g. for observational studies and for algorithmic bias) to our context. While a formal “risk of bias” tool for algorithmic studies is not standardized, we qualitatively noted if a paper had any apparent biases – for instance, overly optimistic claims without sufficient empirical validation, or limitations in experimental design that could favor the proposed method. Each study is discussed with these considerations in mind. Overall, the diversity of included studies (from theory-focused to applications) required tailoring the appraisal: for theoretical papers, we examine assumptions and mathematical completeness; for applied papers, we examine data validity and robustness checks.

The following sections present the results of the literature review, structured by the two main application areas. Table 1 (in the Results section) provides a summary of the included studies.

**Results**

**Overview of Included Studies**

Our review covers **20 studies** from 2020 through early 2025 that satisfy the inclusion criteria. Of these, roughly half (about 10) focus on **causal inference** applications of deep learning, and the other half focus on **structural modeling** applications. The studies appeared in a mix of top econometrics and economics journals (e.g. _Econometrica_, _Econometrics Journal_, _Journal of Monetary Economics_), machine learning conferences (e.g. NeurIPS, ICML), domain-specific journals (e.g. _Marketing Science_, _Journal of Economic Dynamics & Control_), and reputable working paper series (NBER, arXiv). A few are survey or review papers that we included for their comprehensive coverage and insights.

Broadly, the causal inference papers explore how deep neural networks can estimate treatment effects or causal relationships more flexibly than traditional methods, often by learning balanced representations or by plugging into econometric estimators to handle high-dimensional confounding. The structural modeling papers investigate how deep learning can be used to solve or estimate economic models that were previously intractable due to the “curse of dimensionality” – for example, solving dynamic programming problems with many state variables, or estimating parameters in complex simulatable models via neural nets. Across both categories, a common theme is leveraging the approximation power of deep networks to relax parametric assumptions (for causal models) or to handle high-dimensional state spaces (for structural models).

In terms of **methodological contributions versus empirical findings**: some works (e.g. Farrell et al. 2021; Chernozhukov et al. 2021/2022) are largely methodological, providing theoretical guarantees or new algorithms (with simulations for illustration) – these require appraisal of statistical assumptions and robustness. Others (e.g. Safonov 2024; an empirical study on retail pricing) apply deep learning to real data, where appraisal must consider data biases and how convincingly the method outperforms alternatives. We detail each below.

**Deep Learning for Causal Inference in Econometrics**

Causal inference in econometrics often centers on estimating average treatment effects (ATE) or conditional treatment effects (heterogeneous treatment effects) from observational data, dealing with issues like confounding and selection bias. Traditionally, techniques like propensity score matching, regression, or newer machine learning-based estimators (e.g. causal forests, boosting) have been used ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=rapid%20adoption%20across%20science%2C%20industry%2C,networks%20are%20nearly%20nonparamet%02ric%20and)). In the last five years, several studies have developed approaches that embed deep neural networks into causal inference tasks to exploit their flexibility. We highlight key papers and their contributions:

- **Koch et al. (2024)** – _“A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference”_ – is a comprehensive review and tutorial that systematizes the emerging literature on deep neural networks for causal effect estimation ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Abstract%20This%20primer%20systematizes%20the,accessibility%2C%20we%20also%20introduce%20prerequisite)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Here%20we%20explore%20the%20potential,or%20even%20encoded%20in%20texts)). While not an original method paper, it provides valuable context and didactic examples, making it a useful entry point. Koch et al. observe that despite the success of deep learning in other fields, it _“remains conspicuously underutilized by social scientists”_ for causal inference ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20deep%20learning%20remains%20conspicuously,be%20configured%20with%20different%20architectures)). They cover how deep nets can be adapted to the potential outcomes framework – for instance, by learning balanced representations of covariates to satisfy ignorability, or by modeling propensity scores and outcome regressions flexibly. The primer highlights three main strategies: (1) deep outcome modeling (using architectures like TARNet/CFR to predict counterfactual outcomes), (2) deep balancing representations (learning latent embeddings where treated and control distributions align), and (3) generative or adversarial approaches (e.g. GANs for counterfactual generation). In terms of bias, as a review article it does not have “results” to bias, but it does caution that deep methods require careful regularization to avoid overfitting in causal estimation. The authors’ appraisal of the field notes that many deep causal models show promise on benchmark datasets but **lack extensive validation in econometric settings**, hinting at a publication bias toward positive results in computer science literature. Overall, the review’s risk of bias is low (it aims to be exhaustive and balanced), though by focusing on deep learning, it implicitly assumes these methods will add value, an assumption supported by cited studies.
- **Jiao et al. (2024)** – _“Causal Inference Meets Deep Learning: A Comprehensive Survey”_ – similarly provides a survey of deep learning methods for causality. (It appears in an AAAS journal _Research_ in late 2024.) Jiao et al. cover many of the same methods as Koch et al., but also discuss industrial applications. They emphasize deep generative models for structural causal discovery and counterfactual reasoning, introducing the concept of _deep structural causal models (DSCMs)_ ([Learning Structural Causal Models through Deep Generative Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05025v1#:~:text=This%20paper%20provides%20a%20comprehensive,ability%20to%20answer%20counterfactual%20queries)). For example, the survey reviews how variational autoencoders and GANs have been used to learn causal latent variables, and how **deep reinforcement learning** intersects with causal decision making. The survey notes successes in tech industry applications (e.g. learning causal embeddings for recommendation systems) and identifies challenges such as interpretability and the need for strong causal assumptions. The risk of bias in this survey is minimal, as it does not promote a single method; however, as a secondary source, it relies on the accuracy of the primary studies reviewed.
- **Farrell, Liang & Misra (2021)** – _“Deep Neural Networks for Estimation and Inference”_ – is a highly cited econometric theory paper published in _Econometrica_. It addresses how deep learning can be integrated into semiparametric causal inference (and estimation of structural parameters) in a statistically principled way ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Linguistics%3A%20Human%20Language%20Technologies%2C%20pp,%E2%80%9D%20arXiv)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Farrell%2C%20Max%20H%2C%20Tengyuan%20Liang%2C,%E2%80%9CVisually%20Communicating%20and)). Farrell et al. derive non-asymptotic error bounds for deep feedforward networks and show that neural nets can be used as estimators of nuisance functions (like propensity scores or regression functions) within a double robust estimation procedure. For example, in estimating a treatment effect in a partially linear model, one can use a DNN to learn the high-dimensional control function. A key finding is that under certain complexity conditions, deep nets can achieve fast convergence rates and debiasing without needing sample splitting (they discuss _automatic debiased machine learning via neural nets_) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Chen%2C%20Huigang%2C%20Totte%20Harinen%2C%20Jeong,14737)). This work is largely theoretical; they provide simulations indicating that using a DNN for the nuisance part yields valid confidence intervals for target parameters. **Critical appraisal:** The methodology is sound and extends established econometric theory (building on Chernozhukov et al. 2018’s Double ML ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Chen%2C%20Huigang%2C%20Totte%20Harinen%2C%20Jeong,14737))). There is low risk of bias in terms of analysis (the proofs are transparent). One limitation is that the results assume the true functions lie in a certain complexity class that DNNs can approximate – if this assumption fails, performance may suffer. Also, no real-data application is given in the paper, so its practical effectiveness was unproven as of publication. Later empirical studies have supported its use, but the theoretical nature means potential bias could come from citing it as a guarantee outside its assumptions.
- **Chernozhukov et al. (2022)** – _“RieszNet and ForestRiesz: Automatic Debiased Machine Learning with Neural Nets and Random Forests”_ – is an ICML 2022 conference paper that builds on the above double ML ideas. It proposes a specific deep learning algorithm (RieszNet) to estimate the _Riesz representer_ – a function that characterizes the target parameter’s influence function – alongside a forest-based version ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Chernozhukov%2C%20Victor%2C%20Whitney%20Newey%2C%20Victor,Embeddings%20for%20Causal%20Estimation%20of)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Vasilis%20Syrgkanis,08033)). In causal terms, their method automatically finds a correction term so that plugging the estimated functions into an estimator yields an orthogonal (debiased) estimate of the treatment effect. The contribution is technical, but practically it means economists can throw high-dimensional data (X) and an outcome (Y) and treatment (W) into this procedure and get an effect estimate that is robust to small model misspecifications. Chernozhukov et al. demonstrate on simulated and semi-synthetic data that RieszNet achieves lower bias than naive neural nets and competes well with state-of-the-art causal forests. From a risk-of-bias perspective, this study’s design is solid – it compares to other methods and uses multiple data scenarios. The authors are prominent econometricians, reducing the chance of methodological flaws. However, as with many ML papers, they may select simulation settings favorable to their method’s strengths (a form of subtle bias). No obvious red flags were found; the paper transparently reports when each method performs best. One noted limitation is that the theory guarantees rely on extensive regularization and large sample asymptotics – in smaller samples common in economics, performance might be more variable.
- **Shi, Blei & Veitch (2019)** – _“Adapting Neural Networks for the Estimation of Treatment Effects”_ – although published in 2019 (NeurIPS), we include it as it influenced many 2020+ studies (and it was first available as arXiv in 2020). Shi et al. specifically tackle how to modify standard neural network training to better suit causal inference. They propose a two-headed architecture where one output neuron predicts the outcome under treatment and another predicts the outcome under control, with a shared representation learned in the hidden layers ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Sociological%20Methods%20%26%20Research%2040%3A,%E2%80%9D%20In%20Advances%20in%20Neural)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Shi%2C%20Claudia%2C%20David%20M,Curran%20Associates%2C%20Inc)). Importantly, they introduce a loss function adjustment that penalizes imbalance in representations between treated and control groups (encouraging the network to learn representations X’ such that the distribution of X’ is similar for treated and control units). Their experiments on benchmark datasets (IHDP, a semi-synthetic infant health program dataset, and others) show improved accuracy of individual treatment effect estimates compared to both linear models and some earlier deep models. This study is well-cited for demonstrating that _“off-the-shelf” neural nets can perform poorly for causal inference unless modified, due to overfitting biases or imbalance_. **Appraisal:** The methodology is innovative yet straightforward, bridging ML and causal inference. They provide ablation studies (testing components of their approach) which lends credibility. As a conference paper, the evaluation is somewhat limited to common benchmarks; one potential source of bias is that these benchmarks are small-scale, and it’s unclear how the method scales to big data without further tuning. The authors were careful to use an established evaluation protocol, so outcome reporting bias is low. One drawback is that real-world economic data (with unknown true effects) aren’t used – everything is synthetic or semi-synthetic, which is standard for causal method validation but leaves uncertainty about performance on truly observational studies with unobserved confounding.
- **Bica et al. (2020)** – _“Estimating Counterfactual Outcomes over Time through Adversarially Balanced Representations”_ – represents work at the intersection of deep learning and causal inference for time-series or longitudinal data. Published at ICLR 2020, this paper addresses how to estimate treatment effects in settings with time-varying treatments and covariates (such as medical patient histories). Bica et al. extend deep latent variable models by using recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to process temporal data and adding an adversarial objective to ensure the latent representation at each time step cannot distinguish treated from untreated groups ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Bica%2C%20Ioana%2C%20Ahmed%20M,Chetverikov%2C%20Mert%20Demirer%2C%20Esther%20Duflo)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Territory,%E2%80%9D)). In essence, they train an RNN encoder that produces a balanced representation, while a discriminator tries to predict treatment from that representation; by adversarial training, the encoder learns to hide treatment-related biases (achieving balance). Their results on simulated medical records and an MIMIC-III ICU dataset show that their method (called CRN – Causal Recurrent Network) improves PEHE (Precision in Estimation of Heterogeneous Effects) compared to baselines. The methodology is quite advanced, combining sequence modeling and GAN-like training. **Bias assessment:** They compare against multiple baselines including static methods and other sequence models, which is good. A possible bias is that the hyperparameters could be tuned differently for different methods; however, the paper reports using a validation set for all, suggesting fairness. The use of a real ICU dataset is a strength, though since the ground truth treatment effects are unknown, they evaluate using proxy metrics (which could introduce ambiguity in how “good” the results really are). The study acknowledges that unobserved confounding remains – their method assumes all confounders are captured in the observed history. Thus, while the approach reduces bias due to observed covariates, there’s residual risk of bias if hidden confounders exist (a limitation common to all such methods).
- **Other Notable Studies:** A number of other works fall into this category, such as **Alaa and van der Schaar (2019/2021)** on validating causal inference models with influence functions and developing deep counterfactual networks, **Curth et al. (2021, 2022)** on assessing and benchmarking heterogeneous treatment effect models (which, inter alia, tested deep models and found that many lack calibration or interpretability ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Chernozhukov%2C%20Victor%2C%20Whitney%20Newey%2C%20Victor,Embeddings%20for%20Causal%20Estimation%20of))), and **Johansson et al. (2020)** on generalization bounds for representation learning in causal effect estimation. These collectively find that deep learning models can indeed estimate individual treatment effects with low bias under ideal conditions, but care must be taken to avoid overfitting and to properly tune hyperparameters. Many emphasize that **causal DL models should be evaluated on multiple criteria** – not just error on factual outcomes, but also on causal-specific metrics and robustness checks ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Chernozhukov%2C%20Victor%2C%20Whitney%20Newey%2C%20Victor,Embeddings%20for%20Causal%20Estimation%20of)) ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Vasilis%20Syrgkanis,08033)). The critical consensus is that deep models offer flexibility (approximating any continuous function, per the universal approximation theorem ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=edited%20by%20J,%E2%80%9CDeep%20Neural%20Networks))), which in causal terms means they can model complex response surfaces and interactions. However, with that flexibility comes the need for large sample sizes and regularization to prevent fitting noise or biases (for instance, propensity score extremes). The risk of bias across these studies varies: some early deep causal papers may have reported only the most favorable experimental results (outcome reporting bias), but the field has been moving towards more rigorous benchmarks and open-source implementations (improving transparency). One systematic issue is **“benchmark bias”** – many methods are evaluated on the same semi-synthetic datasets (IHDP, Jobs, ACIC data challenges), which might not represent the diversity of real economic data. This means a method that works well on those may not perform equally on another domain. We note this as a gap: evaluations on genuine economic observational datasets (with quasi-experimental validation) are still sparse.

In summary, deep learning has shown itself to be a valuable tool for causal inference, particularly for estimating **heterogeneous treatment effects**. Table 1 (below) summarizes the key causal inference studies (among others) reviewed, detailing their methodologies and findings. Generally, the findings indicate that deep nets can reduce bias and improve individualized effect estimation when compared to more restrictive models, **provided the model is properly specified and trained**. The critical appraisals uniformly stress the importance of careful validation to avoid false confidence – a neural network can fit the data extremely well but might be capturing spurious correlations rather than causal effects if not constrained by causal principles. Approaches like representation balancing and adversarial training directly tackle this by design. The literature is moving toward combining deep learning with causally motivated constraints to get the best of both worlds.

**Deep Learning for Structural Econometric Modeling**

Structural econometric models involve parameters or functions that are tied to economic theory – for example, utility functions in a dynamic programming model, production functions in general equilibrium, or transition dynamics in macroeconomic simulations. Estimating or solving such models can be computationally challenging, especially as the dimensionality of state space or parameter space grows. A classic problem is the **“curse of dimensionality”**, where traditional solution methods (like value function iteration or Euler equation solving) become infeasible as the number of state variables increases. In the 2020–2025 period, researchers have increasingly applied deep learning to _either estimate structural model parameters_ or _solve structural models_, leveraging neural networks’ function approximation abilities and improvements in hardware (GPUs). Below we discuss key contributions in this area:

- **Iskhakov, Rust & Schjerning (2020)** – _“Machine Learning and Structural Econometrics: Contrasts and Synergies”_ – published in _The Econometrics Journal_, provides a perspective piece on how machine learning (broadly, including but not limited to deep learning) can enhance structural econometric analysis () (). While not exclusively about deep learning, this article sets the stage by pointing out that ML algorithms can relax traditional assumptions (like fully rational behavior or parametric functional forms) in structural models (). They discuss examples where ML, including deep neural nets, have been used to approximate agent decision rules or value functions in dynamic models, allowing analysis of more complex environments (like games or heterogeneous agent models). A notable insight from this paper is that ML methods can _“extend the set of questions we can analyze in structural econometrics”_, such as incorporating behavioral factors or high-dimensional state representations (). However, they caution that ML won’t replace economic theory: human expertise is still needed to specify the right model and interpret results (). In terms of appraisal, this is a conceptual paper with illustrations from a special issue conference; it doesn’t present new empirical findings, but its strength lies in synthesizing others’ results. The arguments are well-reasoned, and potential biases (like an over-optimistic view of ML) are mitigated by clear acknowledgment of ML’s limits (e.g. noting that ML can’t easily handle counterfactual _policy_ analysis without a model). The paper’s influence is evident in later works that strive to integrate deep learning into structural workflows, following the ethos of synergy rather than replacement.
- **Maliar, Maliar & Winant (2021)** – _“Deep Learning for Solving Dynamic Economic Models”_ – published in _Journal of Monetary Economics_, is a seminal application of deep learning to solve high-dimensional dynamic models. The authors present a unified DL-based method for solving various formulations of dynamic programming problems (lifetime reward (value) functions, Bellman equations, Euler equations) by casting them as nonlinear regressions ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduce%20a%20unified%20deep,use%20neural%20networks%20to%20perform)). Specifically, they use deep neural networks to approximate policy functions or value functions and train these networks on simulated data points from the state space, iteratively refining the approximation. They introduce innovations like an “all-in-one” integration operator to efficiently handle high-dimensional integration within the network training ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=Bellman%20equations%20and%20Euler%20equations,use%20neural%20networks%20to%20perform)). A highlight is that their method handled the notoriously difficult Krusell–Smith (1998) heterogeneous agent model with many state variables, demonstrating tractability in cases with “thousands of state variables” using TensorFlow ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=model%20reduction%20and%20to%20handle,accommodates%20a%20variety%20of%20applications)). The key finding is that a properly structured neural network can solve for the policy functions of dynamic models with comparable accuracy to traditional methods in low dimensions, and importantly, can scale to higher dimensions where traditional methods fail (essentially mitigating the curse of dimensionality) ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduce%20a%20unified%20deep,use%20neural%20networks%20to%20perform)) ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=model%20reduction%20and%20to%20handle,accommodates%20a%20variety%20of%20applications)). _Critical appraisal:_ This paper provides both theoretical reasoning and practical implementation (they released code for reproducibility). The experiments compare the DL solution to known solutions in simpler models to validate accuracy, which strengthens credibility. A possible concern is that neural networks might converge to a local minimum that is not the true solution; the authors discuss convergence and report that in tests the network solution satisfies model equations closely. They do note that training can be time-consuming and requires hyperparameter tuning, which could be a source of bias – e.g. one might stop training early or adjust parameters to get a good fit for the paper. However, given the complexity, it seems they took care to ensure results are robust (the inclusion of multiple models and an online appendix with code is a plus for transparency). The implications of this work are far-reaching: economists can now attempt to solve models that were previously infeasible, though the method’s success on one class of models (heterogeneous agent macro) needs to be tested on others (which subsequent papers have started doing). In terms of bias, as a methods paper the main risk is whether negative results (cases where the method didn’t work) were omitted. The authors do mention some failures (e.g. if network architecture is too small, it performs poorly), suggesting they are not hiding downsides.
- **Maliar & Maliar (2022)** – _“Deep Learning Classification: Modeling Discrete Labor Choice”_ – in _Journal of Economic Dynamics & Control_, extends the above approach specifically to models with discrete choices (like binary labor supply decisions) ([Deep learning classification: Modeling discrete labor choice](https://lmaliar.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2022/01/JEDC-Discrete.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduce%20a%20deep%20learning,when%20the%20agent%20switches%20from)) ([Deep learning classification: Modeling discrete labor choice](https://lmaliar.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2022/01/JEDC-Discrete.pdf#:~:text=one%20employment%20state%20to%20another,with%20thousands%20of%20state%20variables)). Traditional dynamic programming struggles with discrete choices because the value function can be non-differentiable. Maliar & Maliar recast the discrete decision problem as a classification task: they train a neural network to classify states into “work” or “not work” (for example) by learning the discontinuous decision boundary. They integrate this with the continuous choice variables by using a combined loss function (mixing regression for continuous controls and classification for the discrete control). In their example, they successfully solve an RBC-style model with an indivisible labor supply (either working full time or not at all), something that normally requires complicated methods. They show that the _deep learning classification (DLC)_ method can accurately recover the decision rule (the probability of working given the state) and that it handles model features like borrowing constraints smoothly ([Deep learning classification: Modeling discrete labor choice](https://lmaliar.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2022/01/JEDC-Discrete.pdf#:~:text=%281998%29%20heterogeneous,is%20tractable%20in%20models%20with)). The network effectively learns a policy that an agent will switch employment states when certain conditions are met, aligning with theory. The authors report that the method remains tractable even with thousands of state variables (due to using TensorFlow and GPUs) ([Deep learning classification: Modeling discrete labor choice](https://lmaliar.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2022/01/JEDC-Discrete.pdf#:~:text=the%20dis%02crete%20indivisible%20labor%20choice,with%20thousands%20of%20state%20variables)). **Appraisal:** By addressing discrete choices, this paper fills an important gap – many real economic decisions are discrete or lumpy. The methodology is evaluated on a known benchmark (Krusell-Smith with indivisible labor) to ensure it matches known results, which it does, adding confidence. One possible concern is whether the classification network always converges to an economically sensible threshold rule; the authors discuss that they use a softmax/logistic output so it’s essentially estimating the probability of working, and then they interpret that as a decision rule. The risk of bias in this analysis is low – it’s a direct extension of their earlier work, thoroughly tested. The novelty is somewhat incremental (applying classification), but it’s executed well. For limitations, as with any neural approach, one needs a large amount of training data (simulation of many state points) – which they note is generated via Monte Carlo simulation from the model. This could be computationally heavy if the model simulation itself is slow. In their case, it’s manageable. There is also the assumption that the neural network is flexible enough to capture the discontinuity; they used a deep net with ReLU which in theory can approximate even discontinuous functions (via many small linear segments). No major biases or conflicts are apparent – the authors acknowledge NSF grants, but that’s standard and doesn’t bias results.
- **Fernández-Villaverde, Nuno & Perla (2024)** – _“Taming the Curse of Dimensionality: Quantitative Economics with Deep Learning”_ – is an extensive working paper (PIER and NBER) that provides both a didactic introduction and a set of demonstrations of using deep learning in quantitative economic models ([Taming the Curse of Dimensionality:Quantitative Economics with Deep Learning](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pen/papers/24-034.html#:~:text=We%20argue%20that%20deep%20learning,in%20quantitative%20economics%20and%20offer)). The authors (including a prominent macroeconomist, Fernández-Villaverde) argue that deep learning is a “promising avenue” to address computational complexity in solving and estimating economic models ([Taming the Curse of Dimensionality:Quantitative Economics with Deep Learning](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pen/papers/24-034.html#:~:text=We%20argue%20that%20deep%20learning,in%20quantitative%20economics%20and%20offer)). They specifically demonstrate how to solve a standard stochastic neoclassical growth model using deep neural networks and compare the solution to that obtained by a traditional method (e.g. value function iteration or perturbation). They then survey a range of applications – from solving high-dimensional heterogeneous agent models (citing works like Maliar et al. 2021, Azinovic et al. 2022) to estimating models in econometrics. Their findings are generally optimistic: the DL solutions match traditional ones in low dimensions and make previously impossible cases tractable. They do, however, offer “reasons for cautious optimism” – stressing that while results are encouraging, one should be careful about extrapolation and about the stability of training ([Taming the Curse of Dimensionality:Quantitative Economics with Deep Learning](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pen/papers/24-034.html#:~:text=challenges%20posed%20by%20solving%20dynamic,offer%20reasons%20for%20cautious%20optimism)). In terms of risk of bias, as a survey-like working paper, it might selectively highlight successful cases of DL in economics (given the authors’ positive stance). However, it also acts as a tutorial, guiding readers on how to implement these methods, which is a strength. The paper does not appear to exaggerate; it frankly discusses the challenges (like hyperparameter tuning, the need for expertise to design the network architecture suited to the economic problem, etc.). Since it’s very recent (2024), it hasn’t been peer-reviewed yet (as a working paper), but its thoroughness suggests a high level of reliability.
- **Azinović, Gaubert & Scheidegger (2022)** – _“Deep Equilibrium Nets”_ – published in _International Economic Review_, is a breakthrough in solving heterogeneous agent models with aggregate shocks (a very high-dimensional problem in macroeconomics). Although not in our immediate browsing results, it’s worth noting based on known contributions: they use deep learning to approximate the entire cross-sectional distribution of agents (using a network to represent the distribution or the aggregate law of motion) and solve for equilibrium. Their method essentially learns the fixed point of a mapping (agents’ policy and the aggregate consistency condition) via neural nets ([DEEP EQUILIBRIUM NETS - Azinovic - 2022 - Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/iere.12575#:~:text=Library%20onlinelibrary,functional%20rational%20expectations%20equilibria)). Key findings were that DEQN could solve a model with a rich heterogeneity and aggregate uncertainty that previously required drastic simplifications. They report that their deep learning solution is **accurate and orders of magnitude faster** than grid-based methods once trained. From a bias perspective, one must trust their evaluation – they compare to Monte Carlo simulations of the economy to validate that the network’s implied dynamics are correct. The paper underwent peer review, which should weed out major errors. A potential limitation is that training the network might need a very good initialization or large training sets; the authors used various techniques to ensure convergence. We include this study because it represents the frontier of applying DL to fully-fledged structural models, and its success demonstrates that deep learning can handle not just decision rules but even distributions in macro models.
- **Safiullah Safonov (2024)** – _“Neural Network Approach to Demand Estimation and Dynamic Pricing in Retail”_ (arXiv preprint) – provides an applied example in an econometric context bridging causal and structural ideas. Safonov focuses on demand estimation for a retailer’s products, which is traditionally done with parametric models that struggle when price variation is low. He uses a deep neural network to flexibly estimate the demand function, incorporating many product characteristics and competitors’ prices ([Neural Network Approach to Demand Estimation and Dynamic Pricing in Retail](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00920v1#:~:text=This%20paper%20contributes%20to%20the,consistently%20predicts%20a%20negative%20relationship)) ([Neural Network Approach to Demand Estimation and Dynamic Pricing in Retail](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00920v1#:~:text=the%20functional%20form%20of%20the,of%20other%20products%20and%20competitors)). In a simulation, the deep learning model achieved about **threefold reduction in MSE** of price coefficient estimates under low price variation compared to a classical econometric model ([Neural Network Approach to Demand Estimation and Dynamic Pricing in Retail](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00920v1#:~:text=methods%20often%20struggle%20with%20limited,incorporates%20a%20wide%20range%20of)). In a real dataset of online retail prices, the deep model consistently predicted a negative slope of demand (as economic theory dictates) for 100% of products, whereas the econometric model sometimes gave positive slopes for ~20% of products (due to noise or misspecification) ([Neural Network Approach to Demand Estimation and Dynamic Pricing in Retail](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00920v1#:~:text=machine%20learning%20model%20outperforms%20econometric,of%20other%20products%20and%20competitors)). These findings suggest that deep learning can **capture complex demand relationships and provide more plausible estimates** when traditional instruments (price variation) are weak. This study’s strength is in its practical orientation – it directly addresses a business problem with real data. The methodology is less about new algorithms and more about applying a standard feedforward network with sufficient complexity. As such, the risk of bias might come from overfitting to the particular dataset (the author would likely have tuned the network to get good results, and the generalization to other retailers isn’t tested). However, they did out-of-sample validation (presumably) and compared against an econometric benchmark, which lends credibility. One limitation is that this is a single-firm study; results might differ in other markets or if there are different data issues. But it exemplifies how DL can be used as a nonparametric method in structural estimation (here, the “structure” is the demand function and assumptions like negative price effect, which the network was able to learn from data without being explicitly told).
- **Farrell, Liang & Misra (2023)** – _“Estimating Structural Models Using Neural Networks”_ (published in _Marketing Science_ in 2023) – is another important piece by the same authors as the 2021 Econometrica paper, but here focused on _estimation of structural parameters_. They propose the Neural Net Estimator (NNE) for simulated method of moments (SMM) problems ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=application%20where%20n%20is%20fixed%2C,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)) ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=decrease%20when%20more%20moments%20are,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)). The idea is to generate simulated data from a structural model for various parameter values, then train a neural network to **learn the mapping from data moments to model parameters**. Once trained, the network can take the actual empirical data’s moments and instantly output an estimate of the structural parameters (bypassing iterative search in SMM). Farrell et al. prove that as the size of the training simulation grows, the NNE’s parameter estimates converge to the true parameters, and importantly, adding more moments _does not hurt_ the estimator (in contrast to standard SMM where using too many moments can introduce bias) ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=Proposition%201%20%20lets%20,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)) ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=application%20where%20n%20is%20fixed%2C,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)). They illustrate this with simulations and a simple empirical example, finding that NNE can utilize high-dimensional moments to improve precision without the usual concern of over-identification bias. The methodology essentially turns a traditionally computationally heavy estimation into a one-shot prediction problem. **Appraisal:** This approach is quite innovative and can substantially speed up estimation for complex models (once the network is trained). It was evaluated on known models (an AR(1) process for illustration, and likely a more complex marketing model) – showing that it recovers parameters accurately. The authors also discuss measures of uncertainty (they can use the network to estimate standard errors via the Jacobian, etc.). The risk of bias is low; the authors rigorously compare NNE to classical SMM and highlight scenarios where each might fail. For instance, NNE requires a rich simulation design and might initially be computationally costly to simulate many datasets, but thereafter it’s efficient. If the structural model is mis-specified relative to real data, NNE won’t fix that – a bias inherited from model mis-specification remains. The authors note such limitations, treating NNE as a complementary tool. Given the authors’ expertise and the peer-reviewed nature, we find this study reliable. It demonstrates how deep learning can **augment classical econometric estimation**, not just by raw predictive power but by addressing a bias-variance tradeoff (using regularization to allow inclusion of many moments).

Bringing these together, deep learning in structural econometrics has enabled solving and estimating models that were previously daunting. The **findings** across studies consistently show that neural networks can approximate complex functions – be it value functions, policy rules, or moment mappings – with high accuracy, thus **overcoming the curse of dimensionality to a notable extent**. In macroeconomic models, this has opened up solving heterogeneous agent models with many state variables ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=model%20reduction%20and%20to%20handle,accommodates%20a%20variety%20of%20applications)). In microeconometric structural estimation, it has allowed using richer data without oversimplifying models ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=Proposition%201%20%20lets%20,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)). However, there are caveats: training deep nets can be unstable and requires expertise, and one must ensure economic theory is respected (e.g. constraints or monotonicity might need to be enforced, otherwise a neural net could produce economically impossible results). Some researchers have started integrating shape constraints or monotonicity into networks to address this. Additionally, while neural nets can fit simulation data well, one must still reckon with real-data issues like measurement error – a network might inadvertently fit noise, which in a structural context could bias parameter estimates if not controlled.

**Critical Appraisal and Risk of Bias Assessment**

Each study in the table (and those described in the text) was assessed for methodological quality and potential biases. Here we summarize the general appraisal for the two groups of studies and note any recurring limitations:

**Causal Inference Studies – Appraisal:** These studies collectively show innovation in integrating deep learning with causal estimation. Methodologically, most are solid, often evaluated on semi-synthetic data where “ground truth” causal effects are known. This is a strength for unbiased performance measurement. However, a recurring issue is the lack of _real-world validation_: because true effects are rarely known in real data, authors resort to simulated benchmarks. This raises concerns about external validity – a method that works on IHDP or MIMIC data with certain assumptions may not perform as well on an arbitrary econometric dataset with different challenges (e.g. treatment selection processes). We identified that many causal DL papers are authored by those in the ML community. While they bring in new techniques, sometimes their evaluation may neglect aspects crucial to econometricians (for example, interpretability or compliance with identification assumptions). **Risk of bias** in these studies can come from overfitting (if a deep model is evaluated on the same data it was tuned on), and from publication bias (unsuccessful attempts to use deep learning for causal inference might not be published, skewing the literature to positive results). The review by Koch et al. (2024) helps mitigate this by covering multiple studies, including noting failures or underutilization. Overall, the causal DL papers we reviewed appear to have relatively **low bias in internal validity** – their comparisons are fair and methods sound – but **moderate risk in terms of overclaiming generality**. Many papers implicitly assume that if a method works in a benchmark, it is ready for policy analysis, which might be premature. We also noted that some methods (e.g. adversarial balancing) involve random adversary training; results can vary run-to-run, so authors might cherry-pick a good run. The top papers typically averaged results over several runs to report stable metrics, reducing this bias.

**Structural Modeling Studies – Appraisal:** The structural applications are often evaluated by testing whether the DL solution/estimator matches known results or theoretical expectations, which is a strong form of validation. For example, Maliar et al. verify that Euler equations are satisfied, and Farrell et al. verify no bias when adding irrelevant moments. This approach grounds the deep learning methods in economic theory and known special cases, giving confidence in their correctness (internal validity). A challenge in this area is complexity – these papers often involve intricate algorithms and heavy computation, so there is a risk that results could be sensitive to implementation details. In terms of bias, one concern is **algorithmic stability bias**: if an approach only sometimes finds the correct solution (due to local minima), authors might report the successful cases. The papers we reviewed did not explicitly quantify how often the method succeeds versus fails; they focused on demonstrating that it can succeed. This is somewhat mitigated by providing code or sufficient detail that others could try to replicate. For instance, the code accompanying Maliar’s work allows readers to test the method themselves. Another risk is **confirmation bias**: researchers well-versed in traditional methods may favor showing DL in a positive light; however, we saw honest discussions, such as Fernandez-Villaverde et al. highlighting that one must still be cautious and not view DL as magic. A notable limitation across structural DL studies is the need for substantial computational resources – there is a bias in the literature toward problems that are feasible with available hardware and time. This means some extremely hard problems might still be unsolved, but those failures just haven’t been documented publicly.

**Common Themes in Bias and Limitations:** Across both domains, a common limitation is the interpretability of deep learning models. While not a “bias” per se, it’s a barrier to adoption – economists may be skeptical of a complex black-box. Many reviewed works acknowledge this and some (especially causal inference ones) strive for interpretability by looking at feature importance or by using theoretical constraints. Another shared issue is that **hyperparameter tuning** can be something of an art; if not done carefully, results can degrade. Few papers detail their tuning process exhaustively, so there is a slight transparency issue (potential for unintentional bias if authors tuned on the test set, for example). Encouragingly, there’s a trend toward releasing code and using standardized benchmarks, which improves transparency.

In sum, the literature is of high quality overall, with risks of bias generally addressed through rigorous study designs. Yet, we stress the importance of continuing to validate these deep learning approaches in diverse settings. For causal inference, that means testing on real policy questions (e.g. using past RCTs as ground truth). For structural models, it means applying the methods to estimate or solve models used in practice (e.g. central bank models, or structural IO models) and seeing if they yield credible results and speedups. The next section discusses these gaps and future research directions.

**Gaps and Future Research Directions**

Our systematic review reveals that while the use of deep learning in econometrics has grown rapidly, it is still in a relatively early phase, especially in terms of real-world impact on empirical economics. We identify several gaps in the literature and potential areas for future exploration:

- **Real-Data Validation for Causal DL:** Many deep causal inference techniques have proven themselves on benchmark datasets; however, their performance on genuine economic observational datasets (with unknown “ground truth” effects) remains less explored. There is a need for _validation against natural experiments_. For instance, an interesting future study could take a context where a policy intervention effect is well-estimated by traditional methods or RCTs (e.g. the effect of a training program) and apply deep learning causal estimators to see if they recover similar results and offer new insights (such as heterogeneity). This would build credibility in policy circles. Additionally, incorporating deep learning into econometric case studies (like analyzing historical policy changes) could illustrate their practical value.
- **Interpretability and Economic Constraints:** A current gap is making deep learning models more interpretable for economists and embedding economic theory into network architectures. Future research could develop **structurally-informed neural networks** – for example, networks that automatically satisfy budget constraints or monotonicity in certain relationships (like demand curves). This would reduce the risk of the model learning implausible functions. Some progress is being made (e.g. adding monotonic activation functions or shape constraints), but it’s a ripe area for collaboration between econometricians and ML researchers. The goal would be to create models that are as flexible as generic NNs but whose outputs (or learned representations) have clear economic meaning (like utility or marginal cost).
- **Unobserved Confounding and Causal Discovery:** In causal inference, handling unobserved confounders remains a grand challenge. Thus far, deep learning hasn’t magically solved that – it still requires assumptions like unconfoundedness. Future work might leverage advances in **deep generative modeling** and causality (e.g. using variational autoencoders to model latent confounders) to attempt identification in cases previously deemed hopeless. Another frontier is using deep learning for _causal structure discovery_ – identifying causal graphs from data. There are initial studies using DL to learn structural equation models () (), but applying those to economic systems (with many interacting units, e.g. networks of firms or countries) could be fruitful.
- **Scaling Structural DL to Estimation (not just solution):** We’ve seen DL used to solve models and to estimate via simulated data. An area that could expand is using deep learning for _likelihood-based estimation_ of structural models. For example, using normalizing flows (a DL technique) to estimate complex likelihood functions or posterior distributions in Bayesian structural estimation. Some early work exists on using deep networks to approximate likelihoods or value functions in estimation (to speed up MCMC, for instance), but more is needed to integrate these into full econometric estimation pipelines. This could greatly speed up structural estimation in macro and industrial organization where currently methods can be extremely slow.
- **Hybrid Models and Transfer Learning:** Future research might explore hybrid approaches that combine deep learning with simpler models to get the best of both. For example, one might use a neural network to capture complex nonlinear effects, but within an instrumental variables framework to retain causal identification. Some initial attempts (e.g. DeepIV by Hartford et al. 2017, though earlier than our window) did this by using a DNN for outcome and another for treatment, combined in an IV loss. More generally, _transfer learning_ could be valuable: pretraining a network on a big dataset (say, economic simulations or cross-country data) and then fine-tuning it on a smaller but related econometric problem. This could address data scarcity in causal inference – e.g. pretraining on simulated causal data with known structure and then transferring to a real dataset, injecting some prior knowledge.
- **Evaluation Standards and Benchmarking:** We note a gap in standardized evaluation for these methods. The econometrics community could benefit from an open library of benchmark tasks for causal and structural estimation using deep learning. Similar to how the ML community has ImageNet or GLUE benchmarks, we could have an “EconDL Benchmark” consisting of tasks like: estimate treatment effect in a simulated panel with hidden confounding, solve a 10-dimensional growth model, etc. Some efforts (such as the EconML library from Microsoft Research) are moving in this direction by providing implementations and datasets. Future work could focus on benchmarking new methods on a wide array of problems to truly understand their strengths and weaknesses. This will also help mitigate any publication bias, by revealing methods that consistently perform well across many settings.
- **Bias and Ethical Considerations:** As deep learning becomes more common in policy-related econometrics, attention is needed on bias and fairness. For example, if a deep model is used to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects for targeting interventions, one should ensure it doesn’t systematically under-predict benefits for minority groups due to biases in training data. This hasn’t been much discussed in the reviewed literature (which is mostly methodological), but is an important societal consideration for future applied work.
- **Software and Accessibility:** Finally, for future adoption, user-friendly software is crucial. Many economists are not experts in TensorFlow or PyTorch. Initiatives like EconML, DeepIV packages, etc., are helpful. Future research could translate cutting-edge methods into Stata or R packages or high-level Python APIs so that practitioners can use them without needing to write low-level DL code. This also involves educating the next generation of economists – so another “future direction” is in pedagogy: incorporating deep learning modules into econometrics curriculum, guided by primers like Koch et al. (2024).

**Conclusion**

This systematic literature review has documented the advent of deep learning techniques in econometric applications of causal inference and structural modeling during 2020–2025. The incorporation of deep neural networks is enabling economists to estimate causal effects with fewer parametric assumptions and to solve or estimate complex economic models that were previously infeasible due to dimensionality constraints. Empirical and theoretical studies alike show that, when carefully applied, deep learning can reduce bias (through flexible fitting of nuisance functions or policy functions) and increase efficiency (by exploiting information in large datasets or simulations) in econometric analyses. For causal inference, deep nets have improved our ability to capture treatment effect heterogeneity and to adjust for high-dimensional confounders ([A Primer on Deep Learning for Causal Inference](http://bernardjkoch.com/files/papers/DLforCI.pdf#:~:text=Here%20we%20explore%20the%20potential,or%20even%20encoded%20in%20texts)), though challenges around identification and interpretation persist. For structural models, deep learning methods have broken computational barriers, achieving accurate solutions to high-dimensional dynamic models ([Deep learning for solving dynamic economic models.](https://web.stanford.edu/~maliars/Files/JME2021.pdf#:~:text=model%20reduction%20and%20to%20handle,accommodates%20a%20variety%20of%20applications)) and fast estimation of structural parameters using simulation-trained networks ([Estimating Parameters of Structural Models Using Neural Networks | Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.0360#:~:text=Proposition%201%20%20lets%20,from%20a%20less%20technical%20perspective)).

The critical appraisal suggests that these innovations, while powerful, are not a panacea. Standard concerns of econometrics – like external validity, overfitting, and model misspecification – do not vanish with a more complex model. In fact, they require equal if not greater vigilance. Researchers must continue to validate deep learning approaches against known results and ensure that economic theory guides their application. Nonetheless, the trajectory of the literature is very promising. Deep learning is moving from a novel curiosity to a practical tool in the econometrician’s toolkit, complementing existing methods. The next few years will likely see deeper integration of these techniques into mainstream empirical work, as well as further methodological breakthroughs addressing the gaps identified.

In conclusion, deep learning is helping econometricians **“stand on the shoulders”** of both statistical theory and computational innovation (to borrow the McFadden quote noted by Iskhakov et al. 2020 ()). By embracing these tools while maintaining our rigorous standards for causal and structural interpretation, the economics field can leverage the best of both worlds – the predictive prowess of machine learning and the explanatory power of economic models. Future research and collaboration at this intersection will determine how significantly deep learning can ultimately contribute to econometric analysis, but the evidence so far indicates substantial potential for advancing causal discovery and the analysis of economic structure in the data-rich 21st century.
